There is a particular quality to light between six and eight in the morning that exists at no other hour. It arrives low, warm, and almost liquid — it spills over ridgelines and fills valleys the way water fills a vessel. Photographers speak of "golden hour" as if it were a single window, but the morning has two acts: the first, when the sun is still below the horizon and the sky glows from underneath; the second, when the light finally clears the ridge and every surface is lit from the side.

## Learning to be early

I did not learn to love morning light by reading about it. I learned by missing it. For years I arrived at locations at ten, eleven, noon — when the light is flat, hard, and unforgiving, and the landscape looks the way it will look in a hundred photographs taken by a hundred other people.

The discipline of the early start changed more than my photographs. It changed how I see. To be at a place before the light arrives is to watch the world wake up, and the camera becomes almost secondary.

> The best photograph of the morning is not the one you planned — it is the one you were patient enough to let happen.

## What the morning teaches

Morning light is generous with texture. It rakes across stone, reveals the bones of a landscape that midday flattens into silence. Shadows become long and legible; distances become tangible.

It also forgives. When the light is soft, small mistakes in exposure and composition go unnoticed. The morning is the one time a photographer can work quickly and still think slowly.

## A practical note

If you want the light, you need to want the hour. Check the forecast, arrive early, and allow the first photographs to be failures. The light builds slowly — so should you.
