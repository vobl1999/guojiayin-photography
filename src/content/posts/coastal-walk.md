Some days the coast gives you everything — wind, salt, a low tide that opens the shoreline like a page. This was one of those days. I walked with a single lens, a decision that has never once failed me. One lens means no decisions about equipment; it leaves only decisions about seeing.

## The discipline of one lens

With one focal length, the photograph becomes a question of position. You cannot zoom your way into an idea. You move closer, or you step back, or you accept the distance and let the frame do its work.

The afternoon moved in three acts: the tide pools at low water, the rocks at mid-tide, and the last half hour when the sun dropped behind the headland and the whole shoreline turned to silhouette.

## What I kept

Three photographs. In an afternoon of several hundred frames, three survived the edit. That ratio is normal, and it should not discourage you. The edit is where the photograph is actually made — the shutter is only the beginning.

1. The first: a tide pool holding a perfect reflection of the sky.
2. The second: a line of rocks that repeated into the distance.
3. The third: the sun, a headland, and a single figure walking the wet sand.

## Returning

The coast is patient. It will still be there next month, rearranged by the weather, and the light will be different, and the walk will be a different walk. That is the real reason to return to a place: not to repeat a photograph, but to find out what changed.
